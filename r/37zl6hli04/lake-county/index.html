Lake County, Illinois

Gun violence in the county of Highland Park, Waukegan and North Chicago. This page shows who counts the dead, what officials chose to do after the 2022 parade shooting, what state law requires of everyone, and what research says works.

    Who holds what

    Every number on this page comes from someone who was responsible for something: a licence, a court order, a wounded patient, a record. Four questions help sort out who. The table below follows a gun and the harm it does through ten steps, from the licence to the grave, and answers the four questions at each step.

    1. Who holds it?Name the office or the owner.
    2. Shall or may?A duty everyone carries, or a choice someone makes.
    3. Who keeps the count?And what does nobody count.
    4. Who is left with the problemwhen the holder does nothing.

    A duty is a law that says shall. It applies in every county, so it sets a minimum everywhere. A power is a law that says may. It is used only where someone chooses to use it, so its use varies from county to county. A step marked nobody has no one responsible at all.

      Checking this timeline against the deaths

      A timeline reflects choices about what to include, so this one is checked against the deaths. The first bar shows the 340 firearm deaths the Lake County Coroner recorded from 2018 to 2024, by type. The second shows the 39 entries the timeline had before this edition, by the type of death each one addresses.

      Two fifths of the entries dealt with mass shootings, which caused 3% of the deaths. Sixteen of the 39 entries concern assault weapons and the Highland Park shooting; nine people were killed in the county's mass shootings over those seven years. Three entries concern red-flag orders and safe storage.

      There are fair reasons for the imbalance. The parade shooting wounded 48 people and changed the state's politics. Bans get challenged in court, and court cases produce dated events that fit a timeline; quieter laws produce fewer. The check measures this page's own editorial choices. It says nothing about what the laws achieved. This edition adds two entries that are not gun laws, an election and a hospital, because who enforces the law and who runs the trauma centre also matter.

Four things in the earlier edition were wrong or out of date.

      • The chart of shootings by town counted every incident the archive recorded as a shooting in which someone was hit. Waukegan had 47 incidents, and someone was hit in 25 of them.       • The page called Lake County's 2024 restraining-order rate the highest of any Illinois county over 100,000 people. In 2024 Tazewell County's rate, 7.7 per 100,000 on ten orders, was above Lake's 6.5. The State's Attorney's claim rests on the 2023 and 2024 average of 5.4.
      • The page treated the Waukegan hospital as a trauma centre. The state revoked its Level II designation on 23 October 2026, the second time since 2024, and has sent ambulances elsewhere since 7 November.

      Methods and sources

      Map. Census tracts are the 2019 cartographic boundaries (2010 vintage) with poverty from the CDC/ATSDR Social Vulnerability Index 2018, which is why the base layer is dated 2014 to 2018. Municipal boundaries are 2019 Census places clipped to the county. Shooting points are every Gun Violence Archive incident with coordinates inside the county from January 2014 to March 2018, the span of the last bulk release of the archive's incident feed; suicides are omitted from the map and the counts. Mass shootings are the archive's list of incidents with four or more people shot, 2013 to 3 November 2026, from a daily mirror, plus one 2025 North Chicago incident reported by police that the mirror lacks. Fatal police shootings are the Washington Post's database, 2015 to 2024; 2025 and 2026 incidents in Waukegan and Mundelein reported in the press are not on the map.

      County counts. Coroner figures are from the Lake County Coroner's annual reports for 2018 to 2024; where a report gives the firearm share of suicides rather than a count, the count shown is that share multiplied by the total and rounded. Firearm homicides for 2022 to 2024 are from the State's Attorney's Gun Violence Prevention Initiative newsletter and dashboard bulletin. The coroner counts deaths occurring in the county and includes motor-vehicle homicides in its homicide total, which is why 2019 shows six shooting homicides among 33. Resident suicides and homicides by all methods are the Illinois Department of Public Health's injury-death tables for 2010 to 2023. Five-year firearm, suicide and homicide rates are the National Center for Health Statistics counts released through County Health Rankings 2020 to 2025 (the 2023 release repeats the 2022 window); Illinois and national lines sum the reporting counties. Restraining orders are those entered into the state police LEADS database, as charted by the State's Attorney's office; the 2025 Lake County figure is 101 filings from a Brady press release of 23 April 2026 that cites State Police data, which is a different count from orders entered. Statewide orders received by the State Police are Everytown Research's compilation of state records for 2019 to 2024 and the State Police's 2025 Gun Trafficking report for 2025. County totals for 2024, 254 statewide, are from the state police database as reported through the State's Attorney's office. Affidavit counts are the Illinois State Police's endorsements-per-county table, and its items are firearms, attachments and .50-calibre ammunition together; FOID shares are from CU-CitizenAccess's analysis of State Police data. Rates per 100,000 use 2023 Census population estimates. By-municipality counts are recomputed from the archive's incident records, 2014 to 2017, grouping every incident that is not a suicide by its place and counting separately those in which someone was killed or wounded.

      Trauma distance. Level I and II trauma centres are those the federal hospital file (HIFLD, 2021 designations) lists in and around Lake County, which still lists Vista Medical Center East as Level II. For each of the 74 shootings in which someone was hit, 2014 to March 2018, suicides excluded, this page measured the great-circle distance to the nearest centre, then again with Vista removed, and took medians. Straight-line miles understate drive time. The designations are those in the 2021 file, not those of 2014 to 2018. Vista's 2024 and 2026 revocations are from press reports linked in the policy timeline.

      Illinois maps. The two county maps draw the 102 Illinois counties, with three Wisconsin counties for context. Orders entered in 2024 and registrants are the figures described above, and registrants per 1,000 use 2023 population. Orders are binned by count (none, 1, 2 to 5, 6 to 15, 16 or more) and registrant rates in five classes. A county with no orders entered is left blank, which means the state database shows none, not that data are missing.

      Wisconsin comparison. Death rates for Lake and McHenry counties in Illinois and Walworth, Kenosha and Racine in Wisconsin are the five-year 2018 to 2022 CDC counts with 95% intervals, as in the atlas. Poverty is from the 2014 to 2018 survey estimates in the CDC and ATSDR Social Vulnerability Index. The table of laws is this page's summary of the two states' statutes. The ATF figures are its 2024 firearms trace data for Illinois and for Wisconsin, published by state only, linked below.

      Audit. The attention audit compares the Lake County Coroner's firearm deaths for 2018 to 2024, 340 in all (203 suicides, 137 homicides of which 9 were killed in the county's mass shootings), with this page's timeline entries as they stood before this edition, 39 in all. The coroner's suicide counts for 2021 to 2023 are derived, as described above. Each entry was tagged by hand with the kind of death it is aimed at; the tags are editorial judgement, and the audit's table lists the entries under each.

      Research. Each study's figures are taken from the paper, its journal's press release, or the publishing institution's summary, as linked in the citation; where a summary rather than the full paper was the source, confidence intervals may be absent. The RAND matrix shows only the policy–outcome pairs graded on RAND's own summary page, and the Illinois column is this piece's reading of the statutes. The Everytown rate ladder uses petitions filed, or orders issued where a state reports only those.

      Policy. Dates are signing or decision dates unless marked; effective dates are given in the text. Status is as of 9 November 2026 and was checked against dockets, the General Assembly's bill status pages and press reports linked in each entry. Two items were pending at publication: the Highland Park survivors' appeal in the Second District, and the Supreme Court's AR-15 cases, with argument scheduled for 2 December 2026 and a mootness suggestion filed on 5 November.
